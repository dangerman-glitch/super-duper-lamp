Earlier revisions treated capacity as the risk and a trickle budget as the answer. With a real ceiling of 20 and a real target of nine, that reasoning inverts. You are not at risk of buying demand you cannot serve — you have fourteen empty slots and a number to hit. The risk now runs the other way: spending too little, reaching too slowly, and concluding that paid acquisition does not work for you when what actually failed was the budget.

The redesign

The quiz stops being the destination and becomes the sorter

Pointing a cold ad straight at a booking page asks a stranger for a calendar slot before they know who you are. That is the wrong ask and it is against your whole anti-pressure posture. But Find Your Phase already solves it. The quiz sorts people by where they are — Learn, Improve, Compete — and that maps cleanly onto what you sell:

Nobody is asked to buy anything cold. They answer a few questions about their own lifting, and the ones who are already training seriously get offered a conversation. Everyone else goes into the nurture sequence and stays there until they are ready. Learn to Lift keeps earning quietly in the background — it is second in priority, not switched off.

The creative mix changes with it. Opinion shorts still do the cold work; they are what earns reach. But your athlete competition clips — mean 1,216 views, a smaller and much more qualified audience — become the retargeting layer. Someone weighing up whether you can coach them onto a platform should see Kevin's 12 kg competition PR and Eddie at Worlds. In rev 1 those clips were a footnote. Now they carry the close.

One technical consequence

A trickle budget cannot optimise on booked calls

This is the one real cost of the two decisions, and it is worth understanding before we start rather than discovering it in week five.

Meta's optimiser learns by watching conversions. It needs something on the order of 50 per week per ad set to work properly. Even at $30/day aimed at booked calls you might see one or two a month — nowhere near enough. Point the optimiser at booked calls and it never learns; it will spend your money close to randomly.

The fix is standard and it works: optimise on quiz completions, measure on booked calls. Quiz completions are frequent and cheap enough to be a real training signal. Booked calls are what we actually judge the system by. The two are wired separately — the machine steers by the first number, you and I read the second.

Two smaller consequences of the same arithmetic. Two ad sets, not three — at $30/day a cold set and a retargeting set can both breathe; a third would starve all of them. And judge at 90 days, review monthly — cost per signing needs that long to mean anything, though a month is enough to catch a campaign that is plainly broken.

The system

Four moving parts, one of which is you

Collector

Nightly pull of organic performance from the Instagram Graph API and YouTube Data API into Supabase, which you already have connected. Backfills all 70 videos on first run.

Scorer

Ranks every post on engagement per impression, not raw views, so a strong recent post is not buried by a 2024 clip that had two years to accumulate.

Promoter

Turns an approved winner into a live Meta ad through the Marketing API: campaign, ad set, audience, creative, budget. Later, the same step against Google Ads for YouTube.

Governor

Enforces the daily cap, auto-pauses anything above your cost ceiling, and — new in this revision — pauses everything when your slots are full. Stopping spend never needs approval.

The capacity switch is the important addition. You tell the system how many open slots you have. It counts booked calls against that number and shuts the ads off when you are full, then turns them back on when someone finishes a block or moves on. This is what makes aiming at 1:1 safe rather than reckless — the machine physically cannot generate more demand than you have room for.

Where you sit in it, as decided: once a week you get a short list — “these three posts earned promotion, approve?” Launching costs money, so you say yes. Pausing saves money, so the machine does it alone, with no approval and no delay.

Which reframes what the ad system is for. Paid acquisition into a bucket that empties every six months is the more expensive way to grow, and with only six people in the bucket the leak is proportionally enormous.

I am not recommending we drop the ad build; you have decided on it and the payback case below holds. I am saying the ads should not be the only thing you change this quarter, and if you ever have to choose between the two, retention wins on the arithmetic.

Worth knowing what the contrast implies. You convert and hold in-person athletes at roughly ten times the volume of remote. Whatever you do face-to-face — the accountability, the immediacy, the room — is not currently reaching your remote athletes. That is a coaching-delivery question rather than an advertising one, but it is the thing most likely to move remote LTV, and no amount of ad spend substitutes for it.

One piece of that is mechanical, not motivational. Two of the twelve cancellations ended in payment_failed — involuntary churn, an athlete who did not choose to leave. And in the last 90 days, 3 of 22 charge attempts failed, all the same customer whose card keeps returning transaction_not_allowed. Dunning — automatic retries and a card-update email — recovers most of that. It is the cheapest revenue on this page.

The goal, costed

One and a half athletes a month gets you there

First, the calendar. You have taken $6,935 through Stripe so far in 2026, running at about $745/month. If $20k means calendar 2026, that needs $13,065 across four months — roughly $3,266/month, from six athletes to eighteen, starting September. That does not happen, and I would rather say so now than in December.

Read as $20k over the next twelve months, it is very reachable. Here is the model: start at $745/month, add athletes at $200, lose them at your current 11.1% monthly churn (the flip side of a 5.9-month median), cap at 20.

Now the budget. At 1.5/month you need eighteen signings. What that costs depends entirely on cost per acquisition, which nobody knows for your funnel yet:

  • CAC $240 — optimistic. $360/month, $12/day. LTV:CAC of 4.9:1.
  • CAC $400 — a realistic settled figure. $600/month, $20/day. 3.0:1, payback in 2.2 months.
  • CAC $600 — plausible while the system is still learning. $900/month, $30/day. 2.0:1.
  • CAC $800 — the point where I would stop and rethink the offer. Still 1.5:1 and still profitable at your margin, but thin.

So: $25–30/day, not $8. Roughly $750–900 a month. I know that is three times what you asked for, and I am recommending it against your stated preference for one reason — $8/day buys about 0.5 to 1 signing a month at realistic CAC, which is the row where you end the year having gone nowhere. A trickle was the right call when the goal was "keep a slow line arriving." It is the wrong call for a number you have to hit.

The protection is that this stays cheap to abandon. You are not committing $9,000; you are committing to look at cost-per-signing every month and stop if it does not come in under $800. At 90% margin and 2.2-month payback, a working month funds the next one.

The multiplier

Retention is worth more than the ad budget

Same model, same spend, only the churn number changes:

  • 5.9-month median (today): $25,942 collected, 15.1 athletes at month 12.
  • 9-month median: $29,714 collected, 18.7 athletes — +$3,772 for zero extra ad spend.
  • 12-month median: $31,697 collected, and you hit the capacity ceiling.

Moving your median from 5.9 to 9 months is worth almost a fifth of your goal, for free. At today's churn, roughly half of every ad dollar buys a replacement rather than growth — you are paying to refill a bucket instead of filling it.

You already know how to hold people; your in-person athletes stay. Whatever does that — the room, the immediacy, being watched — is not currently reaching remote. If this campaign is the thing you break away on, remote retention is the part that decides whether it is a business or a treadmill.

Money and measurement

What it costs and how we know it worked

$25–30/day, about $750–900/month, reviewed monthly rather than judged at 90 days. Infrastructure is effectively free; Supabase and Zapier are already on your account.

The payback maths, now that I can do it. Your six active subscriptions run $75–$150/month, median $125. Your twelve cancelled subscriptions lasted a median of 5.9 months. So a signed athlete is worth roughly $740 — about $1,000 at the top of your price range with a better-than-median stay.

That gives the test one clean question, asked every month: is cost per signed athlete under $800? Under $400 and you are on track for $20k. Between $400 and $800 it still pays at your margin but the goal slips. Above $800 for two consecutive months and we stop and fix the offer rather than spend into it.

Three metrics, in order. Do not skip ahead:

  • Cost per quiz completion — the optimiser's steering signal, not a business result. Under $4 means delivery is healthy.
  • Quiz → booked call rate — the number that tells us whether the routing works. If quizzes are cheap but nobody in the Improve or Compete phase books, the problem is the offer or the routing, not the ads, and we fix that before touching budget.
  • Cost per signed athlete, against the $1,062 of contribution an athlete carries — the only number that decides whether this continues.

Honest risks. Meta Business verification can take two weeks and is outside my control. Early CAC will look bad — the first six weeks are the optimiser learning, not the verdict, and you should expect to feel alarmed around week four. And there is a real chance the answer is no: that cold paid traffic does not produce remote athletes at a price that works. Finding that out for a few thousand dollars, before you break away rather than after, is a legitimate and valuable result.

Before I build

Two decisions, then I build

  1. Approve $25–30/day, or hold at $8? This is the one place I am pushing back on a decision you already made, and only because the goal arrived after the budget did. At $8/day the model lands near $10k — a year of work to stand still. If cash flow is the constraint rather than caution, say so and I will build a ramp instead: start at $15/day, and raise it only out of revenue from athletes the ads actually signed. Slower to $20k, but it never costs you money you have not already earned.
  2. Price new athletes at $200? Take the top of your range. At $200 you need 8.3 concurrent athletes for $20k; at $170 you need 9.8. With capacity at 20 you are not short of slots, so the scarce resource is signings, and every dollar of price is a signing you do not have to buy. Leave your existing six where they are — with a book this small, losing one to a price rise costs more than the increase gains.

One thing I want to flag, not ask. The funnel this plan runs on — Find Your Phase, Learn to Lift — sits under Myriad Weightlifting in everything I can see. If you may break away, be deliberate about whether those assets travel with you. Building Dangerman Training Systems' acquisition on a quiz and a program that belong to the gym would mean handing back your funnel at exactly the moment you need it. Worth resolving before Phase 03 puts spend behind it, and cheap to resolve now — a Dangerman-branded quiz is a weekend, not a rebuild.
